Building a realistic plate tectonic simulator for procedural generation requires translating real-world geophysics into lightweight computer science algorithms. Most implementations (ranging from indie world-generators to academic simulators like GPlates) follow a multi-step pipeline.
The primary algorithms and data structures used to simulate plate tectonics include:

1. Plate Partitioning (The Voronoi / Cellular Approach)
Before plates can move, the surface of the map or sphere must be divided into discrete tectonic plates.
Voronoi Diagrams / Lloyd’s Relaxation:
How it works: A set of random seed points (plate "roots") are scattered across the world. Using a Voronoi diagram, every pixel or mesh tile is assigned to its closest seed point, creating natural, organic-looking polygonal plates. Lloyd’s Relaxation can be applied to make the plate shapes more uniform and prevent tiny, splintered plates.
Mesh Representation (Half-Edge / Dual-Connected Edge List):
How it works: On a 3D globe, developers often start with a subdivided icosahedron (or a Fibonacci/hexagonal grid). To track how boundaries shift and collide, algorithms use a half-edge data structure, allowing the program to cleanly track neighboring faces, borders, and vertex splits.

2. Plate Kinematics (Euler’s Fixed-Point Theorem)
Real tectonic plates do not just slide flatly across a 2D plane; they rotate across the curved surface of a sphere.
Euler Poles and Angular Velocity Vectors:
How it works: In spherical simulations, each plate is assigned an Euler Pole (a point on the globe acting as the axis of rotation) and a rotational speed.
Using the right-hand rule, every point on that plate is given a velocity vector pointing in the direction and speed it is drifting.

3. Boundary Interaction Algorithms (The Core Logic)
As plates drift, their edges interact with neighboring plates. The simulation checks the relative velocity vectors of adjacent border cells to classify the boundary into one of three geological types:
Convergent Boundaries (Crashing Together):
Oceanic vs. Continental: The denser oceanic plate subducts (dives underneath) the continental plate. The algorithm carves a deep ocean trench and buckles the continental edge upward into a mountain range (like the Andes).
Continental vs. Continental: Neither plate can subduct because they are both buoyant. The algorithm heavily compresses and uplifts both edges, creating massive, folded mountain chains (like the Himalayas).
Oceanic vs. Oceanic: One subducts under the other, creating deep trenches and volcanic island chains (like Japan or the Aleutian Islands).
Divergent Boundaries (Pulling Apart):
How it works: The plates drift away from each other. The algorithm lowers the elevation at the boundary, creating rift valleys or ocean basins, and fills them with new, low-lying oceanic crust.
Transform Boundaries (Sliding Past):
How it works: Plates grind laterally past one another. The algorithm introduces high-frequency noise, jagged shear lines, and minor elevation offsets (mimicking fault lines like the San Andreas Fault).

4. Crustal Aging and Isostasy
Real planets feature two distinct types of crust: thick, buoyant continental crust (granite) and thin, dense oceanic crust (basalt). Advanced tectonic simulators track these properties over time:
Isostatic Adjustment: Based on Archimedes' principle, heavier crust sinks lower into the mantle (forming ocean floors), while lighter, thicker crust floats high (forming plateaus and continents).
Thermal Subsidence (Age-Dependent Depth): As oceanic crust moves away from a divergent boundary (where it was born hot and buoyant), it cools, contracts, and becomes denser over simulation "steps," causing it to gradually sink deeper underwater the older it gets.

5. Diffusion and Smoothing Passes
Raw tectonic calculations result in very sharp, blocky, or unnatural geometric boundaries because of the underlying grid or Voronoi cells. Post-processing algorithms are run to clean up the map:
Thermal/Hydraulic Relaxation: Spreading out the sharp cliffs generated by plate collisions using iterative height-averaging or cellular automaton smoothing.
Coordinate Domain Warping: Passing the tectonic grid coordinates through a low-frequency noise function to warp straight, artificial-looking fault lines into organic, winding mountain ranges.
